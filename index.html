🔬 Investigational peptide explainer

B7-33: a simplified relaxin, aimed at scarred tissue

B7-33 is a lab-built, single-chain peptide modeled on a fragment of the human hormone relaxin-2. In animal studies it shows anti-scarring (anti-fibrotic) signals — but it has never been tested in people and is not a medicine anywhere. This page maps what it is, how it's thought to work, and exactly where the evidence stops.

🧪

Strictly preclinical — zero human trials

Everything here comes from laboratory and animal research. There are no human safety data, no approved uses, and no established way to take B7-33. Animal findings do not translate directly to people.

01

What B7-33 is

A trimmed-down copy of a natural hormone — built for the lab, not the pharmacy.

B7-33 is a synthetic single-chain peptide — a deliberately simplified version of human relaxin-2 (also called H2 relaxin), a natural hormone involved in tissue remodeling and blood-vessel function.

Researchers built it by taking part of relaxin's B-chain and re-engineering it into a shorter, linear (straight-line) molecule that is far easier to synthesize than the full hormone. Preclinical concept

It is studied mainly in laboratory and animal models for potential anti-fibrotic (anti-scarring) effects — and it is not an approved medication in any country.

The one-sentence version: B7-33 is "relaxin, distilled" — the fragment researchers cared about, rebuilt as one simple chain for easier production and study.

Simplified by design · two chains become one

Native relaxin-2 is a two-chain, folded hormone. B7-33 keeps only the B-chain portion researchers wanted, rebuilt as one straight chain. Conceptual explanation

02

The design idea

Why rebuild a hormone instead of just using it?

Natural relaxin-2 is biologically interesting but structurally awkward: two chains, folded and cross-linked — expensive and fiddly to produce, and harder to study cleanly.

B7-33 is the answer to a design question: what is the smallest piece that might keep the useful biology? By isolating the B-chain segment and straightening it into a linear peptide, researchers got a molecule that is simpler to synthesize and simpler to test — while aiming to preserve the receptor signaling they cared about. Mechanistic interpretation

Relaxin-2 (two chains)→ keep B-chain fragment→ B7-33 (one linear chain)
The bet being tested: a trimmed, easier-to-make peptide that keeps the anti-fibrotic signaling of the parent hormone — without the full molecule's complexity.
03

A century of relaxin

The backstory the remix inherits — including a phase-3 disappointment.

🤰1926 — a pregnancy hormone. Relaxin is discovered for its role in childbirth — loosening ligaments and blood vessels. For decades, that was the whole story.
🫀2000s — a second act in the heart. Researchers find relaxin receptors in the heart and vessels, and a new idea forms: a natural hormone that fights fibrosis and relaxes vessels could help failing hearts.
🧪2010s — serelaxin's mixed finale. Recombinant human relaxin-2 (serelaxin) reaches phase-3 trials for acute heart failure. Results are mixed; no approval follows. The idea survived — the drug did not. Human trials (parent drug)
🧬2010s–20s — the remix is engineered. B7-33 takes the same relaxin–RXFP1 biology and compresses it into a single chain, trying the hypothesis again with a simpler molecule.
Why this history matters: serelaxin's failure doesn't prove B7-33 can't work — but it proves that promising relaxin biology doesn't automatically become medicine. The remix inherits the hypothesis, not the results.

From childbirth hormone to heart-failure hope — and the remix

Relaxin started as a childbirth specialist, got scouted for cardiology, flopped its big audition — and now its remix is trying out for the same role. Conceptual explanation

04

Biased signaling at RXFP1

Not just "on" — selectively on. This is the core pharmacology idea.

Both relaxin and B7-33 act through RXFP1 (relaxin family peptide receptor 1). The native hormone switches on several downstream pathways at once — useful, but broad.

B7-33 is described as a biased agonist: it preferentially drives the ERK1/2 pathway, which raises production of enzymes such as MMP-2 that break down excess collagen — while only weakly triggering the cAMP pathway. Mechanistic interpretation

The hypothesis: anti-fibrotic payoff with fewer of the broader effects seen with full relaxin. Selective signal, narrower ripples.

Why "biased" matters: receptors are like switchboards with multiple outgoing lines. A biased agonist doesn't just flip the master switch — it favors one line over the others, aiming for the desired outcome with less collateral signaling.

One receptor, two lines · the bias

B7-33 is thought to favor the ERK1/2 line (collagen-clearing enzymes up) while barely nudging cAMP — the selectivity behind its anti-fibrotic hypothesis. Mechanistic interpretation

06

What the evidence actually supports

Great family, no personal achievements. Sorted by tier — honestly.

Established — relaxin's biology (not B7-33's)

Relaxin is a real hormone with real roles: tissue remodeling in pregnancy, vasodilation, RXFP1 signaling across organs. These are the parent's facts. B7-33 inherits the hypothesis, not the résumé.

Established — the chemistry

Lab assays show B7-33 engages the RXFP1 receptor with preferential pERK signaling. A molecule that fits the lock is a fact — fitting is not the same as healing.

Emerging — animal signals

Anti-fibrotic and cardiovascular hints in rodent models — less scarring, better organ measures, vascular effects. This is the entire evidence base for B7-33. Genuine early science — and early science fails often.

Caution — the parent's phase 3

Serelaxin took this exact biology into phase-3 heart-failure trials and stalled: mixed results, no approval. When the original stumbles at the finish line, the remix starts with humility.

Anecdotal — thin by design

Online chatter about B7-33 is barely existent — and that thinness is itself honest. Thin chatter matches thin evidence. Anecdotal

The report card: great family, no personal achievements. The hormone it copies is famous; the drug it hopes to become flopped; the remix itself has never been tested in a person. Three generations, zero human data points.

08

Safety: knowns and unknowns

The honest version: mostly unknowns — kept in separate boxes on purpose.

No formal human safety data exist. Everything below comes from animal work and theoretical reasoning — not from people taking B7-33.

💉Local injection-site reactions were noted occasionally in preclinical work. Animal study
🩸Transient blood-pressure effects are theoretically possible, since the peptide acts on blood vessels. The mechanism itself implies pressure drops — expected, unmeasured, unmanaged. Mechanistic interpretation
❓Immune reactions and long-term effects in humans remain unknown. There is simply no data. Conceptual explanation
⚠️Anything sold as B7-33 is an unregulated research chemical. No quality oversight, no purity guarantees — for a molecule with zero human data.

Serious adverse events have not been systematically reported in the available animal literature at the doses used for anti-fibrotic research — but with zero human trials, human risk cannot be assessed with confidence, and researchers stress that animal findings don't transfer directly to people.

B7-33 vs the field

The remix against the original — and against lookalikes. Simpler is not proven.

🧬 B7-33 · the remix

  • Structure: single B-chain-derived stretch — engineered for simplicity
  • RXFP1: designed to fit the same lock, with preferential pERK signaling in experiments
  • Status: animals only — carries the essential melody without the orchestra

Human data: none.

🧪 Relaxin / serelaxin · the original

  • Structure: natural two-chain hormone, disulfide-linked — elegant, complex to synthesize
  • RXFP1: the native key — full, natural engagement of the receptor's program
  • Status: decades of biology, a phase-3 heart-failure program, mixed results, no approval

The original reached phase 3 and stalled; the remix has not started.

Not the same as ARA-290

ARA-290 is sometimes mentioned alongside B7-33, but it comes from a different family entirely — derived from EPO (erythropoietin) research, investigated for tissue-protective effects, and acting through very different pathways that don't involve RXFP1. Both are engineered peptides with "protective" storylines, but they speak different molecular languages. Conceptual explanation

Not the same as existing anti-fibrotics either

Approved anti-fibrotic medicines work through different, non-RXFP1 pathways — broad cellular mechanisms, not a relaxin-receptor signal. B7-33's idea is narrower: one receptor, one remodeling message. Narrow can be elegant — it can also miss.

Elegance is not evidence. B7-33 is a cleverer key; whether it opens anything in people is unknown. Relaxin is the acclaimed novel; serelaxin was the adaptation that flopped; B7-33 is the remake nobody has screened yet — and you don't review films before the premiere.

Understanding B7-33 Terminology

Sixteen terms, each with its scientific meaning and a plain-English mirror. Tap each term.

Scientific MeaningB7-33 is a synthetic single-chain peptide engineered as a simplified analogue of human relaxin-2. It was derived from a portion of the hormone's B-chain and restructured into a shorter linear peptide that is easier to synthesize. It functions experimentally as a biased agonist at the relaxin family peptide receptor 1 (RXFP1) and is investigated in preclinical models for anti-fibrotic activity. It has no approved medical use and has not been evaluated in humans.
Plain EnglishB7-33 is a lab-made, single-strand peptide designed as a stripped-down copy of the natural hormone relaxin-2. Scientists took part of the hormone's B-chain and reshaped it into a shorter, straight molecule that is much simpler to manufacture. In experiments it acts as a selective switch for the relaxin receptor and is studied in animals for its potential to reduce scarring. It is not a medicine, has no approved uses, and has never been tested in people.
Scientific MeaningRelaxin-2 is an endogenous peptide hormone of the insulin/relaxin superfamily, first identified in 1926 and originally known for loosening ligaments and connective tissue during pregnancy. Natural human relaxin-2 is a two-chain molecule — an A-chain and a B-chain linked by disulfide bonds — and it signals mainly through the cell-surface receptor RXFP1. Beyond pregnancy, it has been studied for cardiovascular effects including vasodilation, and for anti-fibrotic effects on scar-tissue buildup. Its complex structure makes it comparatively difficult to manufacture, which motivated simplified analogues such as B7-33.
Plain EnglishRelaxin-2 is a natural two-chain hormone discovered in 1926, first famous for its role in childbirth. It works mainly through the RXFP1 receptor and has since been studied for heart and blood-vessel effects, including relaxing vessels and countering scar-tissue buildup. Because its folded two-chain shape is hard and expensive to produce, researchers created simpler stand-ins like B7-33 that try to keep the useful signaling with far less structural complexity.
Scientific MeaningSerelaxin is recombinant human relaxin-2 — the full, natural hormone manufactured as a drug candidate for acute heart failure. It advanced to phase-3 clinical trials (the RELAX-AHF program), where results were mixed: it did not consistently demonstrate the hoped-for benefits, and it was never granted regulatory approval. Its history is the critical cautionary context for B7-33, which is essentially a simpler redesign of the same relaxin–RXFP1 biology that serelaxin tested in people. Serelaxin's failure does not prove B7-33 cannot work, but it shows promising relaxin biology does not automatically translate into an approved therapy.
Plain EnglishSerelaxin was the full natural relaxin hormone, manufactured as a drug and tested in large human trials for acute heart failure. The results were mixed — not good enough — and it was never approved. This matters enormously for B7-33, because B7-33 is a simpler redesign of the very same biological idea serelaxin already tested in people. The flop doesn't prove the remix can't work, but it proves that exciting relaxin science doesn't automatically become medicine.
Scientific MeaningRXFP1 (relaxin family peptide receptor 1) is a G protein-coupled receptor that serves as the primary signaling receptor for relaxin-2. Upon activation it can engage multiple intracellular cascades — including cAMP production and ERK1/2 (pERK) phosphorylation — depending on the ligand and cellular context. It is found in tissues including the heart, blood vessels, kidneys, and reproductive organs, which explains relaxin's wide range of studied effects. Both natural relaxin and B7-33 bind this receptor; B7-33 was designed to engage it with a simpler single-chain structure and a bias toward pERK signaling.
Plain EnglishRXFP1 is the main docking station on cells that relaxin-2 plugs into — and the same receptor B7-33 is designed to activate. It's found in the heart, vessels, kidneys, and reproductive organs, which is why relaxin's effects show up in so many places. Once switched on, it can trigger several internal chain reactions. Which reaction dominates depends on what binds it — which is why a "biased" molecule like B7-33 can favor one outcome over another through this single receptor.
Scientific MeaningA biased agonist is a ligand that, upon binding its receptor, preferentially activates one downstream signaling pathway over others coupled to the same receptor. This contrasts with a balanced (unbiased) agonist, which engages the receptor's pathways in their native proportions. Biased agonism is pursued therapeutically on the premise that favoring a beneficial pathway while minimizing others may improve the benefit-to-side-effect profile. B7-33 is characterized as biased toward ERK1/2 signaling relative to cAMP at RXFP1.
Plain EnglishA biased agonist is a molecule that turns a receptor on selectively — pushing one of its internal signaling routes much harder than the others, instead of activating them all evenly the way the natural hormone would. Drug designers like this idea because it might deliver the wanted effect while dodging unwanted ones tied to the other routes. B7-33 is described as biased toward the ERK1/2 route at the relaxin receptor, with only weak action on the cAMP route.
Scientific MeaningERK1/2 (extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2) are central kinases in the MAPK signaling cascade that relay receptor activation into changes in gene expression and cell behavior. In the context of RXFP1 signaling, ERK1/2 activation is linked to increased expression of matrix metalloproteinases, including MMP-2, which degrade extracellular collagen. B7-33's preferential engagement of this cascade is the proposed mechanistic basis for its anti-fibrotic effects.
Plain EnglishERK1/2 is a key internal signaling chain that carries a receptor's message deeper into the cell, changing which genes get switched on. When the relaxin receptor activates this chain, the cell ramps up production of collagen-dissolving enzymes like MMP-2. B7-33's preference for this exact chain is the leading explanation for why it might reduce scarring: more signal here means more of the enzymes that clear away excess collagen.
Scientific MeaningMMP-2 (matrix metalloproteinase-2, also called gelatinase A) is a zinc-dependent enzyme that degrades components of the extracellular matrix, including collagen. It is a key effector of tissue remodeling: controlled MMP activity clears excess or damaged matrix, while dysregulated activity contributes to disease. Increased MMP-2 expression downstream of ERK1/2 signaling is one proposed mechanism by which B7-33 could reduce fibrotic collagen deposition in animal models.
Plain EnglishMMP-2 is an enzyme that chews up the structural scaffolding between cells, including collagen — the main ingredient of scar tissue. It is one of the body's natural remodeling tools: in the right amount it clears away excess or damaged tissue framework. B7-33 is thought to raise MMP-2 levels through the ERK1/2 pathway, which is how it might dissolve the extra collagen that builds up in fibrotic organs.
Scientific MeaningCyclic AMP (cAMP) is a ubiquitous intracellular second messenger produced when certain G protein-coupled receptors activate adenylyl cyclase. It regulates a wide array of cellular processes, from metabolism to vascular tone. Native relaxin robustly stimulates cAMP production via RXFP1, contributing to its broad physiological footprint. B7-33 activates this pathway only weakly, and that relative sparing is central to its characterization as a biased agonist.
Plain EnglishcAMP is one of the cell's most common internal messengers — a chemical relay involved in everything from energy use to how blood vessels behave. Natural relaxin triggers a lot of cAMP signaling through its receptor, which is part of why the hormone's effects are so wide-ranging. B7-33 only weakly activates this messenger system, and that restraint is exactly what makes it "biased": strong on the ERK1/2 route, quiet on the cAMP route.
Scientific MeaningFibrosis is the excessive accumulation of fibrous connective tissue — essentially internal scarring — in an organ, often as a response to injury, inflammation, or chronic disease. As scar tissue builds up, the affected organ becomes stiffer and less able to perform its function, which is why fibrosis in the heart, lungs, or liver is medically serious. Anti-fibrotic research aims to slow, prevent, or reverse this scarring process, and reducing cardiac fibrosis is one of the main hoped-for effects of relaxin-based research including the animal studies of B7-33.
Plain EnglishFibrosis is harmful scarring inside organs: after repeated injury or long-term inflammation, the body lays down too much collagen and the tissue stiffens, slowly choking off normal function in the heart, lungs, or liver. Since scarring is essentially collagen piling up faster than it gets cleared, one treatment strategy is to boost the enzymes that dissolve it — which is the rationale behind B7-33's proposed anti-fibrotic action.
Scientific MeaningVasodilation is the widening of blood vessels caused by relaxation of the smooth muscle in vessel walls. Wider vessels let blood flow more easily, which lowers resistance and can reduce blood pressure. It is one of relaxin's known effects and part of the rationale for studying relaxin biology in heart failure, where easing the heart's workload matters. However, excessive vasodilation can drop blood pressure too low — a safety consideration for any molecule that relaxes blood vessels, including relaxin-mimetics like B7-33.
Plain EnglishVasodilation is the widening of blood vessels — the vessel walls relax, blood flows more easily, and blood pressure can dip. It's one of relaxin's known effects and part of why researchers studied it for heart failure, where lightening the heart's workload counts. But too much widening can push blood pressure too low, which is why it's a safety watchpoint for any vessel-relaxing molecule like B7-33.
Scientific MeaningHeart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs for oxygen and nutrients. It typically develops from chronic strain — high blood pressure, damaged heart muscle, or valve disease — and leads to fatigue, fluid buildup, and shortness of breath. It was the condition serelaxin was tested against in phase-3 trials, and it is the disease context that B7-33's research story orbits, since anti-fibrotic and vasodilating effects could theoretically help a failing heart. B7-33 itself has never been tested in any human heart condition.
Plain EnglishHeart failure means the heart can't pump hard enough to supply the body's needs — usually after years of strain from high blood pressure, damaged muscle, or faulty valves. It causes exhaustion, fluid retention, and breathlessness. It's the disease serelaxin was tested against (and didn't conquer), and the backdrop for all B7-33 research: if anything could help a stiff, scarred, overworked heart, anti-fibrotic plus vessel-relaxing signals would be the way. But B7-33 has never been tested in any human heart condition.
Scientific MeaningIn peptide and drug science, an analog is an engineered molecule designed to mimic the key effects of a natural molecule while differing from it in structure. Analogs are usually created to be simpler to manufacture, more stable, or more selective in which signals they trigger. B7-33 is a relaxin analog: it keeps the goal of activating RXFP1 but compresses relaxin-2's two-chain structure into a single chain. Importantly, an analog is not identical to its template — structural differences can change which downstream pathways get activated, which is the basis of B7-33's reported pERK signaling bias.
Plain EnglishAn analog is a redesigned copy of a natural molecule — same general job, different construction. Scientists make them to be simpler to build, more stable, or more selective about which signals they trigger. B7-33 is a relaxin analog: it aims at the same receptor but squeezes relaxin's two-chain architecture into one chain. The catch: a copy is never identical to the original, and those structural differences are exactly what give B7-33 its distinct signaling bias.
Scientific MeaningPreclinical research comprises all investigation conducted before human testing: experiments in cell cultures, tissue samples, and animal models. It establishes basic pharmacology, plausible mechanisms, and initial safety signals, and it is a required precursor to clinical trials — but it cannot establish human efficacy or safety. B7-33 exists entirely at this stage: every finding attributed to it comes from laboratory or animal work, with no clinical (human) data whatsoever. Many molecules that look promising in animals fail when tested in people.
Plain EnglishPreclinical means "before human testing" — all the work done in lab dishes and animal models to figure out how a compound behaves and whether it's worth pursuing further. It is a necessary first step, but it can never prove something works or is safe in people, and plenty of animal-stage stars flame out in human trials. B7-33 lives entirely in this zone: everything known about it comes from cells and animals, with zero human studies behind it.
Scientific MeaningPhase-3 trials are the large-scale human studies that test whether a drug candidate actually works and whether its benefits outweigh its risks. They enroll hundreds to thousands of patients and represent the final, most demanding hurdle before a medicine can be considered for regulatory approval. Serelaxin reached phase 3 for acute heart failure and produced mixed results, which is why it was never approved. B7-33 has not entered any clinical phase — it remains entirely in preclinical research.
Plain EnglishPhase 3 is the big leagues of drug testing: huge human trials with hundreds or thousands of patients, the final and toughest exam before a medicine can even be considered for approval. Serelaxin sat this exam for acute heart failure and got mixed grades — which is why it never became a drug. B7-33 hasn't taken any clinical exam at all; it's still doing homework in the lab.
Scientific MeaningPeptide engineering is the deliberate redesign of natural peptide molecules — shortening chains, swapping amino acids, or restructuring them while keeping the parts that drive the desired biological effect. The goal is usually a molecule that is simpler to manufacture, more stable, or more selective than the natural original. B7-33 is a product of this approach: relaxin-2's two-chain structure was compressed into a single chain intended to activate RXFP1 with a bias toward pERK signaling. Elegant engineering does not guarantee a better medicine — the redesigned molecule must still prove itself in testing, and B7-33 has only been tested in animals.
Plain EnglishPeptide engineering is the craft of redesigning natural protein molecules on purpose — trimming chains, swapping building blocks, reshaping them while keeping the parts that do the desired job. The aim is usually something simpler to make, more stable, or more selective than nature's version. B7-33 is a showcase of this craft: relaxin's two-chain hormone compressed into a single chain meant to hit the relaxin receptor with a pERK bias. But beautiful engineering isn't proof of a good medicine — the redesign still has to earn it in testing, and so far that's meant animals only.
Scientific MeaningA placebo is an inactive substance given in clinical trials as a control, so the true effect of a drug can be separated from the effect of simply receiving treatment. The placebo effect — real, measurable improvement driven by expectation, attention, and the ritual of treatment — is why trials compare drugs against placebos rather than against nothing. For molecules like B7-33 that have no human trials at all, the placebo concept matters differently: any unverified report of benefit from an untested molecule cannot be distinguished from expectation effects. That is why anecdotes about untested compounds are not evidence.
Plain EnglishA placebo is a dummy treatment used in trials as a comparison, so scientists can tell what the drug itself did versus what the mere act of being treated did. The placebo effect is real — expectation and attention genuinely make people feel better — which is exactly why proper trials need the comparison. For a molecule like B7-33 with no human trials whatsoever, this cuts deep: any unverified story of benefit can't be separated from expectation. That's the core reason anecdotes about untested compounds don't count as evidence.
